# Learning from Experience

If there's one thing the Navy taught me, it's that experience is the most brutal and effective teacher there is. You can read every manual cover to cover, but nothing prepares you like standing on a flight deck in the Persian Gulf with jet engines roaring and a hundred things that need to go right.

## The Lessons That Stuck

### 1. Comfort Zones Are Where Growth Goes to Die
Every meaningful leap in my career happened when I was uncomfortable. Joining the Navy. Learning to code after getting out. Walking into a coding bootcamp where everyone seemed to already know what they were doing. The discomfort was the signal that I was heading in the right direction.

### 2. Failure Is Data
Early in my software career, I shipped a feature that broke in production. It felt like the end of the world. But the postmortem revealed a gap in our testing process that, once fixed, prevented dozens of future issues. The failure was more valuable than the feature.

### 3. Ask Questions
In the Navy, not asking a question could literally be dangerous. In tech, the stakes are different, but the principle holds. The smartest engineers I've worked with are the ones who ask the most questions. Ego is the enemy of learning.

### 4. Invest in People
The best teams I've been on weren't the ones with the most talented individuals — they were the ones where people genuinely invested in each other's growth. Mentoring, pair programming, honest code reviews. Technical skills are necessary, but trust and collaboration are what make a team great.

## Looking Forward

Every job, every project, every mistake has added something to my toolkit. I'm not the same engineer I was at Groupon, or at Healthbridge, or at Anduril. And I won't be the same engineer a year from now. That's the whole point.

The key isn't to avoid challenges — it's to make sure you're actually learning from them. Write it down, talk it through, teach it to someone else. Experience without reflection is just time passing.
